Why I design

Bad interfaces don't just look wrong. They cost someone their afternoon, their trust, sometimes their pet's health.

Before design was my job title, I sat inside a healthcare operations queue, watching real people get stuck in systems that were never built around them. That's where I learned that most "bad UX" isn't a visual problem — it's a trust problem. People abandon flows the moment they stop understanding what's happening.

I design to close that gap. Not to make things prettier, but to make the next step obvious — so the person on the other end of the screen never has to guess.

That instinct is personal too: when my own pet got sick and I couldn't find the right vet through any app fast enough, I didn't just complain about it — I designed the product I wished existed. You'll find it below.

How I work

From problem to shipped decision

A real, repeatable sequence — not a poster. This is the order I actually move through on every project.

01

Find the friction

Start with a real complaint, not a brief — mine, a user's, or a stakeholder's. I write down exactly where someone gets stuck before I open Figma.

02

Research the gap

Competitor teardown, secondary research, existing support tickets — whatever's fastest to get real signal. I'd rather have three sharp data points than ten vague ones.

03

Sketch wide, then narrow

Paper first. I force myself through several structurally different layouts before touching a design tool, so the first idea isn't the only idea.

04

Wireframe the logic

Low-fidelity screens focused purely on hierarchy and flow — no color, no distraction — so decisions get made on structure, not polish.

05

Design the interface

High-fidelity UI, built as reusable components. Every screen has to justify its own existence against the friction from step one.

06

Pressure-test & ship

Walk it through with fresh eyes, cut what doesn't earn its place, and hand off developer-ready specs — I can build the front end myself when needed.

Self-initiated · Mobile app

VETSSREACH

A veterinary booking app built after I couldn't find the right vet for my own sick pet fast enough.

RoleSolo designer
ScopeResearch → UI

Problem

When my pet got sick, every app I opened made me choose between speed and trust — fast booking with no real vet info, or a slow directory with no way to act. I built VETSSREACH to remove that trade-off.

Context

Self-initiated, not a client brief. I spotted the gap myself while trying to solve a real, urgent problem for my own pet, then designed the product I wished had existed that day.

User pain points

  • No fast way to find a nearby vet with real availability, not just a listing
  • No visible path for emergencies — every flow assumed a routine, unhurried booking
  • Related services like grooming lived in separate apps entirely

Research

Secondary research only — no formal interviews. I audited consultation-booking apps like Practo and similar platforms, reading through their booking flows and app store reviews to find where pet owners specifically got frustrated versus human-healthcare users.

Design decisions

  • Added a dedicated emergency action, always one tap from the home screen
  • Folded grooming and other pet services into the same booking flow instead of a separate app
  • Simplified the visual language against competitors, who I felt buried booking under clutter

Final outcome

A concept app with a streamlined booking flow, a one-tap emergency path, and grooming/services folded into a single experience — differentiated from directory-style competitors on clarity and speed, not just visual polish.

Reflection

his project reinforced that effective UX comes from understanding user needs, not just creating polished interfaces. Every iteration helped improve clarity, usability, and the overall user experience.

Next improvements

In future iterations, I'd validate the booking experience with real pet owners, conduct usability testing, and refine the app based on user feedback. I'd also expand features like vaccination reminders, emergency care, and AI-powered pet health assistance.

Redesign · Travel app

DigiYatra — redesigned

Rebuilding a confusing, low-trust airport onboarding flow into something a first-time flyer can trust in seconds.

RoleSolo designer
ScopeOnboarding → UI

Problem

As a first-time DigiYatra user, I found onboarding confusing enough that I nearly abandoned it — and the visual design made an official government-linked app feel old and almost spammy, undermining trust at the exact moment it mattered most.

Context

A self-driven redesign, triggered by my own first-time-user experience at the airport rather than a client brief.

User pain points

  • Onboarding steps weren't clearly sequenced — unclear what had been completed and what was left
  • Dated visual design read as untrustworthy for something tied to official travel identity
  • No obvious way to get help or use the app in a language other than English

Design decisions

  • Rebuilt onboarding as a clear, linear sequence with visible progress
  • Modernised the visual system to read as official and trustworthy rather than promotional
  • Added a help button linking directly to official DigiYatra information
  • Added a language switcher so users can complete onboarding in their native language

Final outcome

A redesigned onboarding and core flow that reads as calm and official rather than uncertain — with help and language support built in as first-class actions, not afterthoughts.

Reflection

This redesign taught me that trust is a core UX principle for government and utility products—users need clear guidance, transparent information, and confidence at every step. In a future iteration, I'd test the onboarding flow with first-time flyers to validate whether they understand each step, feel secure sharing personal information, and can complete the process without confusion.

Next improvements

I'd validate the redesigned flow with first-time flyers through usability testing, refine the onboarding based on their feedback, and explore features like real-time onboarding guidance, multilingual support, and accessibility enhancements to make the experience more inclusive and intuitive.
